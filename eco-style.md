# The Economist Writing Style: A Practical Reference

This is an English-language synthesis for writing, editing and AI-assisted rewriting. It covers the main principles, qualifications and practical distinctions in Part I. Short examples are taken from, or explicitly adapted from, the book; additional instructions for AI rewriting appear at the end. Historical examples illustrate writing choices, not current events or data. House preferences are identified where they differ from general rules of English.

## The governing idea

**Write as an informed, thoughtful person explaining something to an intelligent friend who does not share all your specialist knowledge.**

The desired voice is conversational but professional, accessible but precise, confident where the evidence permits and candid where it does not. Its authority comes from clear thinking, accurate description and well-chosen evidence.

Keep these priorities in order:

1. Say something true and worth knowing.
2. Make the reasoning and meaning easy to follow.
3. Express them concretely and economically.
4. Add freshness, rhythm and wit where they improve the result.

Economy means making each word earn its place. Sometimes a definition, qualification or example makes a passage longer and better. A short sentence that forces a reader to reread it has saved nothing.

## 1. Words: familiar, concrete and exact

### Prefer the language of ordinary conversation

Short, familiar words usually reach readers more directly than elaborate alternatives. Ask:

- Would I say this word to a friend?
- Will most readers know it immediately?
- Is there a shorter word with the same meaning and appropriate tone?
- Am I choosing it for precision, or to sound impressive?

The book explains this preference through the history of English. Its Germanic foundation supplies many everyday words; French, Latin and Greek contributed much of its formal and technical vocabulary. Familiar Germanic words often feel more immediate because people use them constantly in speech.

This is an explanation, not an etymological purity test. Natural usage and exact meaning take priority. Scientific writing cannot dispense with its established vocabulary, and a familiar French-derived word may be better than a less familiar Germanic one.

Representative preferences from the book:

| Usually prefer | To |
|---|---|
| buy | purchase, acquire |
| let | permit |
| about | approximately |
| enough | sufficient |
| get | obtain |
| help | aid |
| set up | establish |
| spending | expenditure |
| give up | relinquish |
| people | persons |
| but | however |
| after | following |

These are contextual choices, not automatic substitutions. “Acquire” can carry a specific business meaning. “After” cannot replace every grammatical use of “following”. The book's contrast between obtaining a mortgage and getting a beer illustrates how register changes with the situation.

Do not raid a thesaurus merely to vary words. Near-synonyms differ in meaning, implication and register: “thin” and “skinny” do not describe someone in quite the same way.

### Put concrete things back into the sentence

A noun is not necessarily a tangible thing. “Observation”, “process”, “level” and “manifestation” are nouns too, and prose built from them can lose contact with its subject.

Prefer people, objects and actions that readers can picture. When discussing an abstraction, show what it means in the world.

The book contrasts:

> The rise in salaries is not commensurate with consumer-price inflation.

with:

> Grocery bills are growing faster than pay packets.

The second gives readers something to imagine. It is an illustration of the method, not a licence to treat grocery inflation as identical to all consumer-price inflation in every context.

Other suggested moves include “workers” for “workforce”, “pay” for “compensation”, and a specific description of goods for “inventory”. Use them only when the meanings match. Revenue, for example, is not always interchangeable with sales.

Abstract terms remain necessary. “Unemployment” is useful; a paragraph made entirely of levels, trends and indicators may still need to explain what happens to people seeking work.

### Let verbs carry the action

Nominalisations turn actions into nouns: “investigation”, “expansion”, “observation”. They often attract weak supporting verbs such as “conduct”, “undertake” or “exhibit”. Recover the action and put it in the verb.

Examples from the book:

| Wordy or abstract | More direct |
|---|---|
| demonstrates an unwillingness to | refuses to |
| manifests avoidance behaviour | avoids |
| the proliferation of abstractions exhibits a deadening tendency upon writing | empty words deaden writing |

Prefer a precise verb when it carries the meaning of a verb plus an adverb: “strut” may replace “walk proudly”; “murmur” may replace “say softly”. Check the implications before substituting. A vivid verb must still describe what happened.

Concrete subjects and active verbs also expose weak thinking: it is harder to hide a missing actor or an implausible claim when the sentence says who did what.

### Avoid both euphemism and exaggeration

Describe difficult things accurately. Bureaucratic language can conceal violence, hardship or responsibility; dramatic language can inflate ordinary events.

The book suggests replacing broad or sanitised expressions with specific descriptions where the evidence supports them: “lay-offs” for “redundancies”, for example, or the actual conduct behind “human-rights abuses”.

Do not mechanically turn “voting irregularities” into “vote-rigging”, “civilian casualties” into “dead civilians”, or a broad category of misconduct into a specific offence. Those replacements are justified only when the facts establish the narrower meaning.

Likewise, reserve “crisis”, “scandal” and “historic” for events that warrant them. Repeated exaggeration weakens both the words and the writer's credibility.

### Translate jargon; teach the terms worth keeping

Specialist vocabulary can compress a precise idea for insiders. It can also advertise status or obscure what is happening.

Use this sequence:

1. If an ordinary expression preserves the meaning, use it.
2. If a technical term appears only once, consider explaining the idea without naming the term.
3. If the term is essential and will recur, define it in familiar language.
4. On later mentions, use a clear short reference where possible.

The book's discussion of “syntax” and “morphology” illustrates the difference: a general reader may only need “how words are combined” or “how words are built from smaller pieces”.

Acronyms save the writer keystrokes while making readers remember unfamiliar labels. After naming an institution, “the fund” or “the bank” may be easier than repeating its initials. Do not multiply abbreviations merely because they have been defined once.

Treat corporate expressions such as “synergy”, “rightsizing” or “reallocation of resources” as claims to unpack. Explain the actual change when it is known; do not infer a hidden meaning without evidence.

### Be suspicious of fashionable language

The book targets words that are vogueish, self-important, vague or misleading, including “aspirational”, “iconic”, “high-profile”, “paradigm”, “proactive”, “stakeholders” and “transformative”. Its broader test matters more than memorising the list: does the expression add information?

It also objects to fashionable verbs such as “to action”, “to interface” and “to whiteboard”. Forming verbs from nouns is a normal feature of English; the objection is to distracting novelty and office jargon, not to the grammatical process itself.

Word preferences age. Preserve the underlying discipline rather than treating every editorial dislike in the 2023 edition as a universal ban.

## 2. Phrasing, imagery and tone: think afresh

### Replace automatic phrasing with deliberate meaning

Writers absorb the language they read. The danger is assembling familiar phrases without examining the thought they convey.

Scrutinise expressions such as “going forward”, “low-hanging fruit”, “game-changer”, “perfect storm”, “wake-up call” and “tipping point”. State the actual opportunity, change, combination of causes or warning.

The book draws on Orwell's distinction between metaphors that have become ordinary language and those still used as if they were striking. The practical question is whether the phrase helps the reader see or understand anything.

Plain literal language is often the best replacement. Do not turn every cliché into an equally elaborate new metaphor.

### Watch for journalese and prefabricated structures

Journalistic writing has its own stock phrases: negotiations become “marathon” or “make-or-break”; decisions become “landmark”; sources become “key players” or “well-placed insiders”. Such labels can convey atmosphere while withholding information.

The book also criticises ready-made structures such as:

> A is trying to do B. It will not be easy.

If the difficulty matters, explain it. If the next sentence already does that, the announcement may be unnecessary.

The same test applies to headings and cultural references. A familiar song title, film pun or national stereotype is not automatically a good headline. The book points to the repeated use of “Mind the gap” and “No sex please, we're…” as examples of exhausted ingenuity.

### Use metaphors intentionally and consistently

A useful metaphor makes an unfamiliar idea easier to grasp, reveals a relationship or strengthens an argument. It can occasionally organise a whole piece, returning at the end without being forced into every paragraph.

The book uses the blockchain as a ledger as an explanatory example. The comparison helps, but a distributed digital system is not literally a shopkeeper's ledger. Acknowledge an analogy's limits when they matter.

Avoid collisions such as these examples from the book:

> We're in the early innings of this sea change.

> The new product roadmap has hit some turbulence.

Ask whether the image remains coherent if pictured literally. Also ask whether an important difference between the two things undermines the argument. An analogy can explain a claim; it cannot establish the claim by itself.

### Borrow technical language accurately

A term borrowed from music, mathematics or science brings a meaning with it. Do not use specialist vocabulary just to make an ordinary thought sound profound.

The book's examples include:

| Term | Distinction to preserve |
|---|---|
| crescendo | An increase in loudness, not simply the loudest moment. |
| epicentre | The point on the surface above an earthquake's origin, not a grander word for centre. |
| eye of the storm | The relatively calm centre, not the place of greatest intensity. |
| exponential growth | Growth proportional to the current amount, not any rapid increase. |
| inflection point | A change in a curve's concavity, not any reversal or important moment. |
| begging the question | Assuming what the argument needs to establish; use “raises the question” when that is what you mean. |
| parsing | Grammatical analysis in its original technical use; often unnecessary as a grand synonym for examining. |

Use “calculations” or “appearances” when those express the intended meaning more naturally than “calculus” or “optics”.

### Vary language without obscuring reference

Variation can avoid monotony, but a strained synonym makes readers solve a puzzle. The book mocks describing a cucumber as a “cylindrical salad-topper”.

Repeat a clear word when repetition is harmless. Use transparent references such as “the company”, “the bank” or “the condition” when the antecedent is obvious. Keep technical terms stable when changing them would imply a distinction that does not exist.

### Remove redundancy

Words that merely repeat the meaning of their neighbours consume attention. Representative cuts from the book include:

| Longer | Usually sufficient |
|---|---|
| absolute certainty | certainty |
| end result; final outcome | result; outcome |
| in close proximity to | close to |
| located in | in |
| has the ability to | can |
| weather conditions | weather |
| pre-planned | planned |
| the industrial sector | industry |

“Now”, “currently” and “at present” may be unnecessary when the tense already establishes the time. Keep them when contrasting present and past. “Real” is often empty emphasis, but indispensable when it means adjusted for inflation.

Test phrasal verbs in context: a particle may be expendable, or it may change the meaning. These are editing prompts, not search-and-replace rules.

### Combine plain speech with respect

Describe people accurately and with dignity. Ask whether someone in the group would recognise the complexity of the description.

Avoid reducing people to a label. The book prefers descriptions such as “disabled veterans” to a blanket category that erases other aspects of their lives. Include a disability or other personal characteristic only when relevant.

It also recommends “wheelchair user” over “wheelchair-bound”, and “has a condition” over “suffers from” unless suffering is known. These changes improve accuracy as well as tone.

Do not assume that everyone described as a “community” shares an opinion. If the evidence concerns activists, organisations or particular residents, name that group.

## 3. Sentences and grammar: reduce the reader's work

### Keep grammatical structures manageable

Readers temporarily hold unfinished structures in working memory. A long gap between subject and verb, or a clause nested inside another clause, adds a burden before the argument itself can be understood.

- Put the main actor and action near the beginning where possible.
- Keep related elements close together.
- Break up nested propositions.
- Use full stops freely.
- Mix sentence lengths so that brevity does not become a monotonous staccato.

Adapted from the book's example:

**Dense:** Opponents said that the president's declaration that ten ambassadors would be expelled was an attempt to distract the public from economic problems.

**Clearer:** The president said ten ambassadors would be expelled. Opponents accused him of trying to distract the public from economic problems.

Either the action or the criticism can come first, depending on the focus. The important move is separating the propositions and preserving who made the accusation.

### Prefer active voice, with three useful exceptions

Active voice usually identifies the actor directly and uses fewer words:

> Vandals destroyed the shop.

A passive can conceal responsibility when the actor disappears:

> The shop was destroyed.

But passive voice is useful when it:

1. **Preserves focus:** a passage about Lincoln may naturally say “he was assassinated”.
2. **Preserves flow:** something introduced in one sentence can become the subject of the next, moving from known information to new information.
3. **Leaves out an unknown or unimportant actor:** identifying the person who performed the action may add nothing.

Do not confuse grammar with evasiveness. The book's “Someone made mistakes” is active but vague; a passive that names the person responsible can be perfectly explicit. Never invent an actor just to make a sentence active.

### Keep words that prevent ambiguity

Compression becomes counterproductive when readers initially parse a sentence incorrectly.

The book's example:

> The horse raced past the barn fell.

Adding “that was” after “horse” signals the structure before the reader takes the wrong path. Small functional words can be valuable signposts.

Inspect headlines, noun strings, pronouns and modifiers for similar ambiguity. The book's “Swiss Watch Exports Rise” can initially be read either as a statement about watches or as Swiss people watching exports.

The standard is a sentence readers can understand correctly on the first pass.

### Use punctuation to show relationships

| Mark | Main purpose and caution |
|---|---|
| Full stop | Completes a unit of meaning and lets the reader begin again. Make it the default. |
| Semicolon | Connects closely related independent clauses. Do not leave readers guessing whether the relationship is contrast, consequence or addition. |
| Colon | Introduces the explanation, specification or fulfilment promised by what precedes it. |
| Comma | Marks structure and separates elements. Avoid joining independent sentences with a comma alone, except for a deliberate and controlled literary effect. |
| Dash | Adds emphasis or sets off an aside. Its drama fades with overuse. The book suggests no more than one dash or pair per paragraph, and avoiding one in every paragraph. |
| Hyphen | Can show that words function together, as in “interest-rate rises” or “high-school teachers”. |

The Economist's omission of the serial comma is a house convention, not a universal requirement of good prose. Clarity takes priority when adapting this guide to another publication.

### Practical grammar reference

The chapter's recurring principle is that grammar should help readers understand, not force unnatural phrasing in obedience to a dubious prohibition.

| Topic | Working guidance |
|---|---|
| **Who / whom** | Use “who” for a subject and “whom” for an object. Test the relevant clause with “he” or “him”. In “the candidate who we think will win”, “who” is the subject of “will win”; mentally bracket “we think”. Recast if a formally correct “whom” sounds stiff. |
| **Between you and me** | A preposition takes an object pronoun. Adding another person does not turn “me” into “I”. |
| **Subject–verb agreement** | Find the actual subject, not the closest noun. In “Examples from the career of…”, the subject is “examples”, so the verb is plural. |
| **Words ending in -ics** | A discipline such as economics or physics usually takes a singular verb. Other uses and words such as “tactics” may be plural; follow meaning and established usage. |
| **Collective nouns** | Consider whether the noun denotes a unit or its members, and keep usage consistent. The book treats companies, governments and countries as singular; British sports-team usage often takes a plural. |
| **Long subjects** | Do not insert a comma between subject and verb merely because the subject is long. Shorten or restructure it if a pause feels necessary. |
| **Parallelism** | Coordinate matching grammatical forms: “swimming, hiking and biking”, not “swimming, hiking and to bike”. Position “both…and” and “either…or” so that the paired elements match. |
| **Parenthetical information** | Close an aside with its second comma or dash. Do not leave the reader waiting for the main sentence to resume. |
| **Possessives before gerunds** | Both “his snoring” and “him snoring” can be grammatical, with different emphasis. The book prefers the possessive where it fits; it does not treat the alternative as categorically wrong. |
| **Hypothetical subjunctive** | Use “if I were” for an unreal condition. An open question about an actual past event can take “was”: “If he was at the scene…” |
| **Requests and requirements** | American English commonly uses “She demands that each employee arrive…” British prose may use “should arrive” or recast as “She requires each employee to arrive…” |
| **Split infinitives** | They are grammatical. Put the adverb where its meaning is clearest. The book prefers “how to wirelessly hack a car” to an awkward rearrangement made solely to avoid the split. |
| **Sentence-ending prepositions** | They are grammatical and often natural: “the things they were speaking about” need not become “the things about which they were speaking”. |
| **Starting with and, but or or** | Permissible and useful for connecting thoughts. Vary openings rather than applying a blanket ban or repeating the device mechanically. |
| **Dangling modifiers** | Ensure an introductory phrase attaches to the intended person or thing. “Born in poverty, his success…” wrongly makes the success the thing born in poverty. Recast. |
| **Sentence adverbs** | Words such as “fortunately” can comment on the whole proposition. They need not describe the subject's action. |
| **Singular they** | Established English, especially after “someone”, “anyone” and “everyone”. Pluralising or removing an unnecessary pronoun can also solve awkward generic references. Avoid repetitive “he or she”. |
| **Possessive 's** | Expresses relationships beyond ownership. A theatre need not be owned by London for a possessive construction to be grammatical. Use “in London” when location is the intended point and it reads better. |
| **Which / that** | Non-restrictive clauses add information and take commas and “which”. Restrictive clauses identify the referent and can take “that” or “which”; a universal ban on restrictive “which” is unwarranted. |
| **May / might** | Distinguish open possibility, remoteness, reported past speech and counterfactuals. Do not describe an unrealised possibility with wording that implies it actually occurred. These verbs are not numerical probability labels. |
| **Inanimate whose** | Grammatical and often more natural than “of which”. “An idea whose time has come” needs no repair. |

The diagnostic question is whether the reader can identify the subject, action, referent and relationship without backtracking. Recasting an awkward sentence often works better than moving one troublesome word.

## 4. Editing: improve the intended article

### Respect the writer's purpose

An editor's task is to produce the best version of the document the author meant to write. Identify its purpose before changing its structure or voice.

Distinguish explanation from persuasion. An explanatory article need not acquire an editorial position merely because a firmer conclusion sounds more impressive. An argumentative article should make its case clearly, with proportionate evidence.

When working with another writer:

- Agree on the audience, purpose and scope early.
- Identify the passage and the problem, rather than offering a vague dislike.
- Explain what is confusing, unconvincing or slow, and suggest a remedy.
- If the intended meaning is unclear, ask what the writer meant before guessing.
- Consolidate queries where possible.
- Give specific praise for effective explanations, structure or detail so the writer knows what to retain.
- Choose a manageable system for comments and tracked changes.

### Edit in stages

#### Pass 1: purpose and structure

Set the draft aside if time permits. Distance makes it easier to see what is on the page rather than what you meant to put there.

Write a reverse outline: summarise each paragraph's main contribution in a short line. Read those lines alone.

- Does the piece deliver its intended point?
- Does each paragraph advance that point?
- Is any essential step in the reasoning missing?
- Is there a digression that belongs elsewhere or nowhere?
- Is the order easy for the intended reader to follow?

Delete passages that do not earn their place, even if they took effort to write. The book's “murder your darlings” principle is an antidote to the sunk-cost fallacy: effort spent is not a reason to keep a sentence.

A useful thought experiment from the book is to imagine a short editorial based on the reporting. What would it argue? If the original was meant to persuade, does it support that argument? If it was meant to explain, has an unexamined opinion slipped into it?

#### Pass 2: evidence, confidence and fairness

Remove evasive padding when the evidence supports a clear statement. Phrases such as “arguably”, “some say” and “it might be the case that” should express a real evidential limitation, not habitual nervousness.

Retain uncertainty that belongs to the claim. Predictions, disputed reports and estimates must not become certainties during polishing.

The book's joking motto “simplify, then exaggerate” should be understood alongside its insistence on accuracy and fair argument. It is no permission to invent, overstate or erase caveats.

Present the strongest serious objections to an argument. Answer a steel man rather than a straw man. At the same time, avoid false balance: positions with very different evidential support need not receive equal weight.

#### Pass 3: pace and reader support

Move quickly through the straightforward parts; slow down for a difficult mechanism or argument. Add a definition, example, analogy or signpost where it reduces the reader's effort.

Treat a paragraph as a unit of thought, not a fixed number of sentences. Break up long paragraphs when their subjects diverge; use single-sentence paragraphs sparingly.

Consider subheadings, charts and tables when they improve navigation or explanation. Remove background that interrupts the thread merely to demonstrate how much research you have done.

Check that you neither explain the obvious nor assume knowledge the audience is unlikely to have.

#### Pass 4: sentences and voice

Check for:

- real actors and meaningful actions;
- subjects and verbs close enough to follow easily;
- short sentences balanced by occasional longer ones;
- clear hand-offs between sentences, often moving from known to new information;
- nominalisations that can become verbs;
- unexplained acronyms, jargon, dead metaphors and fashionable expressions;
- verbal habits such as repeated “actually” or “thus”;
- overused dashes, semicolons and colons.

Aim for warmth without chumminess, professionalism without stiffness and confidence without swagger.

#### Pass 5: tighten

Test every adjective and adverb. Keep those that distinguish, measure or clarify; remove those that merely advertise importance or enthusiasm.

Examples from the book:

| Draft | Tighter version |
|---|---|
| There was a 23% increase in sales. | Sales increased by 23%. |
| It is not easy to tell. | It is hard to tell. |
| It does not happen often. | It happens rarely. |
| running swiftly | sprinting, if that is the actual action |

Avoid stacked negatives, which can become so tangled that they reverse the intended claim. Prefer an affirmative formulation when it is shorter and equally accurate.

Examine “very”, “really”, “rather”, “somewhat” and “quite”. They may be expendable, vague or dependent on dialect. “Quite”, in particular, can carry different force in British and American English.

Delete promotional superlatives such as “leading”, “best”, “unique” and “innovative” unless they express a supported and relevant distinction. Supply the fact that would justify the praise.

Do not delete all colour. Cutting repetition and padding may create space for a telling detail or quotation that makes the piece memorable.

#### Pass 6: read aloud and proofread

Read aloud to hear awkward transitions, accidental repetitions, missing words and uneven rhythm. Mark every place you stumble.

If possible, ask a reader to identify where they lose the thread and to state the main point in their own words. A response that differs from your intention reveals an editorial problem.

Then check spelling, grammar, names, numbers, units, references and captions. Proofread headings too. A spelling checker helps but may miss an incorrect word that is itself correctly spelled. The book recommends a printout where practical because a change of medium makes mistakes easier to notice.

## 5. Final review checklist

Use these questions before treating a piece as finished:

- **Purpose:** Can I state what this article is doing in one sentence?
- **Reader:** Have I assumed intelligence without assuming specialist knowledge?
- **Structure:** Does each paragraph have a clear job and connect to the next?
- **Agency:** Can readers see who did what, and whose interpretation they are reading?
- **Language:** Are familiar words and meaningful verbs doing most of the work?
- **Precision:** Have simpler expressions preserved the original distinctions?
- **Evidence:** Does the strength of each claim match its support?
- **Fairness:** Have I represented serious objections without creating false equivalence?
- **Numbers:** Are baselines, units, periods, definitions and comparisons sound?
- **Style:** Does each metaphor, joke or striking detail help rather than distract?
- **Economy:** What can disappear without losing meaning, rhythm or useful colour?
- **Readability:** Where would a first-time reader hesitate or need to reread?
